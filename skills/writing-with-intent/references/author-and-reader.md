# Author, reader and angle

Use this when a draft lacks a purpose or consequential human direction. A complete brief or limited edit can skip an interview.

## Ask about decisions, not generic preferences

| Missing input | Useful question | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Angle or position | What do you want to argue or explore, and what makes you uncertain? | Inventing a conviction or asking only for a tone adjective. |
| Reader/occasion | What does this reader already know, and why would they read this now? | A manufactured persona or assuming every piece must drive an action. |
| Experience | Which actual event/detail can you share, and which parts are uncertain/private? | First-person anecdotes fabricated to make prose feel authentic. |
| Evidence | What source or observation supports this claim, and which limit matters? | Treating a desired conclusion as a fact to decorate with citations. |
| Voice/genre | Which qualities of your own sample should survive, and what should change? | Cloning a named author's signature style or flattening idiom into house prose. |

Offer possibilities with comparable specificity and practical consequences. The author can mix, reject or delegate. Do not praise one as thoughtful/authentic and frame the other as bland. A proposed angle should reveal a real tension rather than preselect the answer under a flattering label.

For example, an author writing about a new tool might want a practical explanation, a skeptical argument or a personal account of changing their mind. Those change the evidence and structure. The author's own experience can support the last route only if supplied; a fictional illustration must stay identifiable as fiction.

## Handle partial and delayed direction

Carry the settled part forward. A missing minor preference can use a reversible assumption, while an unresolved personal claim or factual event may require a question or conspicuous placeholder. Do useful independent work while an answer is pending, without issuing the same question through another channel.

When the personal center remains unanswered, keep a provisional draft useful at its honest scope. Leave author-owned gaps conspicuous and develop only what the supplied material supports. Do not fill the requested length by repeating what cannot be inferred or by expanding a neighboring operational topic. Keep a caveat in the prose when it changes the reader's understanding; put remaining editorial uncertainties in the notes. A shorter draft with a clear place for the author's contribution can serve better than a full-length scaffold around its absence.

Attach a delayed answer to its original decision. Retire later questions or proposals based on the superseded premise. An author choosing a restrained technical explanation does not need another vote between a dramatic anecdote and a manifesto. Navigation through a reference gallery is not a lasting preference endorsement.

“You decide” permits a reasoned angle/structure within supplied facts; it does not authorize invented biography or evidence. “I don't know” can be met with a small original passage or concrete alternatives that help the author respond, rather than the same broad questionnaire.

## Keep the deliverable and purpose distinct

An article draft, an editorial assessment and a reusable writing skill are different artifacts. If reviewing an example to improve the skill, record the supported lesson and return to the skill at the trial boundary. Do not turn that into editing the example author's publication, contacting sources or maintaining an unrelated project.

Once intent is sufficient, produce the requested draft or edit. Keep remaining questions limited and visible. Do not require a recurring approval of every paragraph or turn a narrow copy edit into a reporting assignment.
