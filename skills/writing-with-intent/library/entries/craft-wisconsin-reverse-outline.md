## When to use

A draft feels repetitive or its destination differs from the opening promise. Use a complete draft when possible; a partial draft can still reveal local organization. Ask whether the writer wants to preserve the original question or follow an emerging one.

## What to inspect

Inspect the alternative methods: topic sentences, fresh summaries, subject/function pairs, and smaller units of thought. The diagnosis can lead to revising either the paragraphs or the main idea.

## What to borrow and adapt

Give each paragraph two private notes: what it concerns and what it does for the reader. In an article on public transport, two paragraphs might both concern cost while one establishes household stakes and another compares funding choices. They need not be merged merely because their topic labels match. When three paragraphs do the same work, combine or differentiate them; when a necessary inference is absent, add a supported bridge.

## Mismatches and limits

This is a revision aid, not evidence that every successful writer outlines before drafting. Do not invent topic sentences or force all paragraphs into identical units. Narrative scenes, refrains and deliberate recurrences can perform different work across a piece. Paragraph-length comparisons are prompts to inspect, not numerical pass/fail thresholds.
