## When to use

An explanation seems obvious to experts but loses someone approaching the topic now. Ask a specific intended learner what they recognize and where their understanding breaks, rather than treating the writer's own learning history as universal.

## What to inspect

Inspect how Evans distinguishes useful technical terms from empty labels, realistic examples from decorative ones, and a bounded analogy from an elaborate competing model. Her audience discussion includes changing background knowledge over time.

## What to borrow and adapt

For an explanation of retries, begin with one failed operation and the concrete question of what happens on a second attempt. Introduce the relevant state before naming a general abstraction. If an analogy helps explain repetition but fails for side effects, state that boundary. Label a deliberately wrong approach before asking the reader to inspect it; do not let it masquerade as advice.

## Mismatches and limits

This is a practitioner's self-observation with examples, not a controlled comparison of teaching methods. One imagined reader can focus a draft but cannot represent every reader; actual feedback is stronger. Do not invent personal experience, learner testimony or causal reasons to make prose engaging. Not every explanation must start concretely: experts may prefer a formal model or concise definition.
