# Discovery and comparison

Use this mode when the candidate set is open: tools, systems, papers, models, repositories, or approaches to a decision.

## Make the comparison meaningful

Start with the user's decision, candidate unit, constraints, and relevant time/version boundary. Use a short terminology pass to uncover alternate names and approaches before narrowing the search. Choose fields that distinguish candidates for this question; do not impose an exhaustive universal schema.

Apply inclusion rules consistently. Normalize aliases and mirrors to a canonical candidate. Keep forks, versions, and related products distinct when behavior, license, ownership, or release lineage affects the comparison. Explain merge/split decisions when they change conclusions or reported counts.

## Search beyond the first vocabulary

Search multiple relevant query families and source families, including alternative terms and approaches that challenge the initial framing. Use primary materials for technical conclusions. Secondary directories and discussion can lead to candidates or show reported experience, but do not independently establish implementation quality or adoption.

Track successful searches, excluded candidates, and consequential failures at a level appropriate to the requested breadth. For a comprehensive landscape, retain enough search history to explain covered regions and omissions. For a quick comparison, a concise note about source scope is sufficient. A failed query or no result does not establish nonexistence.

## Compare like with like

Build a matrix using the same criteria across included candidates before ranking them. Represent missing evidence as unknown and inapplicable fields as not applicable. Separate documented capability, observed behavior, maturity, adoption, availability, and evidence strength.

For benchmark comparisons, inspect workload, environment, versions, configuration, measurement method, and selection bias. Incompatible conditions support a directional comparison at most. Explain which evidence would be needed for a causal or performance claim. Report license text or SPDX declarations as evidence, not a legal opinion.

## Stop with a defensible boundary

Scale the search budget to the requested breadth and stakes. Stop when relevant terminology and source families have been covered, important counterexamples have been checked, and additional searches yield little useful evidence, or when the available budget or access boundary is reached. For comprehensive requests, state any unmet coverage goals; diminishing returns alone cannot prove exhaustive coverage.

Deliver the comparison, decision implications, and uncertainties. Describe the search boundary, relevant dates, weakly covered areas, inaccessible sources, and ambiguous identities. Say “no eligible candidate found in these searches” when that is what the evidence supports. Do not turn a sampled repository survey into proof of personal usage frequency, effectiveness, or ecosystem-wide prevalence.
