# Use grouping to communicate relationships

## When to use

Use when a screen's relationships are ambiguous: labels seem attached to the wrong controls, repeated cards split one argument, or unrelated actions look like a single unit.

## What to inspect

Read the grouping argument and its commentary together. Distinguish the demonstrated cue interactions from the proposed ordering of perceptual processes. Inspect the original diagrams when using a particular visual demonstration; this card does not stand in for viewing those figures.

## What to borrow and adapt

Choose spacing, alignment, shared surfaces, borders and continuity to communicate the actual content relationships. Ask what people perceive as a group and which control belongs to which object. Use multiple consistent cues when one cue alone is ambiguous.

For a booking form, a shared boundary can bind a workshop's price, schedule and selection control. A boundary around every short sentence may instead imply separate objects that the content does not contain.

## Mismatches and limits

The sources do not prescribe an eight-pixel grid, require cards, or prove a particular CSS layout improves a product task. Competing cues and context can change the grouping. Peterson challenges a theory's serial interpretation, not every practical observation. Verify the intended relationship with the actual content and users rather than calling a decorative pattern scientifically mandatory.
