# Elicit the problem from context, not a questionnaire

## When to use

Use when a project owner knows the situation but cannot yet express a design brief, when stakeholders disagree, or when a familiar interface conceals undocumented workarounds.

## What to inspect

Read the roadmap's elicitation techniques and evolution sections together. In the interview guidance, examine prompts about concrete experiences and adaptive follow-ups. The resources concern different roles: end-user research and an owner's creative direction are not interchangeable evidence.

## What to borrow and adapt

Start from supplied material and an actual occasion of use. Ask what happened, what the person tried to accomplish, and which consequence matters. Use a small sketch to expose an unresolved interpretation when words remain too abstract. Maintain a revisable brief instead of completing a rigid intake form.

For a neighborhood event tool, a story about attending alone may reveal a need for host identity and arrival guidance. That is more actionable than asking whether the page should be “modern.”

## Mismatches and limits

These are methodological resources, not proof that interviews alone uncover every need. People may not articulate tacit behavior accurately; observation and artifacts can contradict stated preferences. Do not copy research-session length or consent procedures into every chat, invent user needs from the owner's taste, or delegate all professional design judgment back to the person.
