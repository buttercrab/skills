# Choose modalities with local users and task evidence

## When to use

Use for a first-time or low-literacy audience, a multilingual field workflow, or a proposed replacement of text with voice or pictograms.

## What to inspect

Read the ethnographic context, task, assistance protocol and participant differences. In the banking comparison, distinguish completion from speed among successful users. Note Wizard-of-Oz speech and experimenter prompts rather than interpreting all completion as unaided success.

## What to borrow and adapt

Investigate the person's language, device familiarity, surroundings, available help and cost of an error. Compare the full task with relevant users. Local graphics, audio, consistent help and human assistance are candidate mechanisms, not an automatic feature bundle.

For a community service, test whether a pictured action means the same thing to participants and whether audio can be heard privately or outdoors. A spoken path can be familiar yet fail if an unfamiliar conversational structure requires the person to infer what to say.

## Mismatches and limits

Historical feature-phone studies do not choose the best modality for today's smartphones or every culture. Text-free does not mean cognition-free; icons are learned and assistance changes outcomes. Do not attach contemporary product claims or a universal voice-first rule to these task-specific findings. The card supplies a research approach, not medical, financial or service-policy advice.
