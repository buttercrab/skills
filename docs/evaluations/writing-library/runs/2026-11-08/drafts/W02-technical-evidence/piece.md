# What Cedar’s Cache Trial Shows

Cedar’s preview service recorded a lower median response time during an hour with its new result cache enabled: 120 milliseconds, compared with 200 milliseconds in an earlier hour with the cache disabled. That is a 40% reduction in the observed median. It is a useful result, but the comparison cannot establish how much of the difference the cache caused. It also says nothing about energy savings, which were not measured. [S1]

Cedar generates image previews from uploaded files. In the trial configuration, it checked an in-memory result cache before rendering. A cache stores a result so the service can reuse it: a “hit” means Cedar reused a previous preview; a “miss” means it still rendered one. This is a result cache inside the service. The supplied description does not establish any change to HTTP caching behavior. [S1]

That mechanism gives us a reason to examine response times: a hit avoids rendering a preview again. It does not tell us how much time a hit saves, or how misses behave relative to the previous configuration. Tuesday’s cache-hit share was 35%. The remaining requests missed the cache and still rendered. The reported latency statistics combine hits and misses; there are no separate latency measurements for those groups. We therefore cannot attribute the overall difference to a measured speedup within either group. [S1]

The two observations cover Monday and Tuesday, each from 10:00 to 11:00. Monday’s cache-off window contained 10,000 successful requests; Tuesday’s cache-on window contained 12,000. The median marks the middle of the recorded latency distribution: roughly half of the successful requests were faster and half were slower. The calculation is straightforward: 200 minus 120 is 80 milliseconds, and 80 divided by Monday’s 200 is 0.40, or 40%. The baseline matters because the percentage describes a reduction relative to Monday’s median. [S1; S3]

That calculation does not mean every request became 40% faster. These are summaries of different sets of requests, rather than before-and-after timings for the same requests. Nor does the median describe the slowest responses. The p95, or 95th-percentile latency, marks the point at or below which roughly 95% of the recorded requests fell. Cedar’s p95 moved from 900 to 840 milliseconds: a reduction of 60 milliseconds, or about 6.7% relative to Monday. The middle and the upper end of the distribution changed by different amounts. [S1]

The request counts need the same care. Tuesday recorded 2,000 more successful requests in its hour, a 20% increase over Monday. That observation does not establish a 20% gain in capacity. We do not know whether the service received comparable demand or comparable work. Input sizes and request mix were not logged. Failed requests were excluded from both rows, and failure counts were not recorded, so these results cannot show whether reliability changed or describe latency across all attempted requests. [S1]

The main uncertainty is attribution. The windows were neither randomized nor paired, and Tuesday may have contained different inputs. The cache could have contributed to the lower observed latency, but this comparison cannot isolate its contribution from differences in the work being requested. No interval estimates were calculated, so the snapshot also provides no quantified uncertainty around the reported differences. [S1; S3]

Birch’s benchmark does not fill those gaps. Birch is a separate service with a different renderer and a disk cache. Its median fell from 250 to 125 milliseconds on a fixed repeated-input benchmark, a 50% reduction. Those measurements contain no Cedar requests. Using Birch’s percentage in a Cedar headline would change the subject while appearing to strengthen the same claim. It is evidence about Birch under its benchmark conditions. [S2]

The proposed energy claim has a separate missing measurement. Less elapsed time is not itself a measurement of energy consumed. The snapshot contains no CPU, memory, power or energy data, and the review comments supply none. Reusing results explains how Cedar can avoid repeated rendering; it does not establish the energy consequence of this trial. [S1; S3]

A practical next measurement would compare cache-on and cache-off behavior on comparable inputs, while recording failures and separating hit and miss latencies. That would help distinguish a cache effect from a change in workload. An energy claim would additionally need direct energy measurements under comparable conditions. For the present article, the supported finding remains specific: Cedar recorded a 40% lower median and about a 6.7% lower p95 across two different hourly windows. [S1]
