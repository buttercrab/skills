# A place for the clamps

In April, I spent two Saturday mornings labeling the shelves in our shared tool cupboard. Mira helped on the second morning. What stayed with me was that, after we'd labeled the shelves, she could find the right clamp without asking me. I liked being less necessary.

That is a small thing to get out of two mornings, but it gives me a better way to explain why the work mattered. The cupboard holds a hammer, three clamps, a hand drill and other tools. Knowing where something belongs is useful information. If that information lives only with the person who arranged things, finding a tool can still depend on finding that person. A label puts some of the information on the shelf, where someone else can use it.

Mira's finding the clamp is the part I can point to. I can't turn it into an account of everyone's experience of the cupboard. But it was enough to make me value a particular kind of usefulness: doing something that leaves another person with one fewer question to ask. I believe a useful routine can make a shared place feel more welcoming. Here, that belief has a very ordinary starting point. Someone could find the tool she wanted.

There is another clamp in my notes, a blue one. On April 13, it was missing from its shelf. On April 20, it was back. I don't know who used it or who returned it. Those two observations leave most of its story untold. Being absent from the shelf doesn't tell me where it was, and being back doesn't tell me how it got there. I didn't feel that its absence proved anything about trust. It would be too easy to give that clamp a meaning its movements don't support.

At the March volunteer meeting, someone proposed a sign-out sheet. No decision was recorded. That proposal raises a different question from the one Mira's experience helps me answer. A sign-out sheet could record use; a shelf label gives a tool a place. Whether a record would help us is still something to consider on its own terms. The blue clamp's unexplained absence and return cannot settle it. Nor does one successful search establish that labels are all the cupboard needs.

I don't want the two mornings to become an argument that everyone should be tidy. A cupboard can have labels without neatness becoming a test of whether someone belongs here. The point I want to keep is more practical: a person looking for a clamp should have a chance of finding it without needing access to somebody else's knowledge of the shelves. Our cupboard is open during staffed sessions, and the tools stay in the building. Within that arrangement, a little information in the right place can be useful.

There are limits to what a label can do. It cannot say whether a tool is being used elsewhere in the workshop, or explain every empty space. It can offer somewhere to look and somewhere to return a tool. That seems a reasonable job for it. The value I saw wasn't a promise that nothing would go missing, or a reason to stop asking each other for help. It was the possibility of getting on with a task when the answer was already there on the shelf.

A place for the clamps is a modest result for those two mornings. For me, its value is in what happened next. Mira found the right clamp without asking me. I had helped make that possible, and for that moment I wasn't needed to explain where it was.
