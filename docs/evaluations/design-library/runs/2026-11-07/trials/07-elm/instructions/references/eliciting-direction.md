# Eliciting direction without taking over

Use this when consequential intent is still unclear or feedback changes the design. These are contextual interviewing practices, not a mandatory interview script.

## Find the decision behind the missing information

| Unknown | Useful move | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary job or audience | Ask for a real occasion, what the person tries to accomplish, and what makes that difficult. | Inventing a persona or asking for a favorite layout before understanding the task. |
| Desired character | Offer relevant contrasting examples or a small local sketch; ask what to keep, change, or avoid and why. | Requiring abstract labels such as “premium” or “minimal” to specify the whole direction. |
| Priority conflict | Describe what each option helps and what it costs under the actual conditions. | Giving one option flattering language and making the alternative sound incompetent. |
| Existing answers | Inspect the brief, assets, current UI, and conversation; ask only about a consequential contradiction. | Re-asking supplied fonts, tone, audience, constraints, or approved choices. |
| Execution detail | Follow project conventions or choose a reversible implementation detail. | Asking the person to select an internal library or every spacing value. |

Ask the smallest question that resolves the design decision. A question's value depends on whether the answer could change the work, who knows it, and the cost of interruption. Some facts are discoverable in the project; some priorities and experiences belong to the person.

## Make room for a direction you did not propose

Alternatives are aids to expression. Explain them with similar specificity and practical consequences. Offer a free answer and accept “neither,” “both,” or an unexpected reference. A recommendation is useful when grounded in known constraints; identify that basis rather than treating personal taste as objective quality.

Ask what matters in a reference: information density, atmosphere, composition, interaction, or particular content. Compare more than one reference when copying surface details would conceal the intended relationship. A single example can anchor the answer. Do not assume parallel options always improve decisions or build multiple complete interfaces by default.

## Handle the person's response

**Partial answer:** carry the answered part into the work. Ask a narrower follow-up only if the remaining uncertainty still changes the outcome. Otherwise declare a reversible assumption and proceed.

**“I don't know”:** reduce the abstraction. Show a relevant pair of local variations or ask which current frustration should disappear. Generate a provisional proposal the person can critique rather than returning the same broad question.

**“You decide”:** accept the delegation. Choose an appropriate direction and execute. Asking the person to pick a style anyway defeats their instruction. Delegation does not supply missing product facts such as who may perform an irreversible action.

**New feedback:** locate the affected decision, propose or make a concrete revision within scope, and preserve everything already settled. Feedback on one heading is not permission to replace the entire brand or flow.

**No response yet:** use the host's pending-question mechanism and continue independent work. Time passing does not answer a required question. Optional reversible choices can proceed as provisional assumptions when the task permits.

## Short original examples

- Vague dashboard request: “What usually brings someone here: finding a booking, comparing the day's capacity, or handling an exception?” Once that is known, visual choices can support it.
- Existing detailed brief: “I’ll retain the supplied typography and warm, practical voice. The missing piece is what should happen when every booking is filtered out.” Ask only if project evidence does not resolve it.
- Creative delegation: “I’m using the neighborhood workshop's materials and repair marks as the visual reference, with the booking task kept direct.” Proceed with the requested artifact rather than turning this into a mandatory selection.
- Ambiguous critique: “The large centered headline and repeated promotional cards are creating that impression. I’ll try a quieter title and show the actual working information sooner.” If two different interpretations would require a major change, ask which one the person means.

Do not claim that the person expressed a preference they did not state. Keep the difference between their answer, observed project constraints, and your proposal visible when it matters.
